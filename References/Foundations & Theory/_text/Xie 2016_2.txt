ABSTRACT
In this paper, we propose a visual servoing control for a quadrotor unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV)
which is based on a state transformation technique. The UAV is equipped with a single downwards
facing camera, and the motion control objective is the regulation of relative displacement and yaw
to a stationary visual target located on the ground. The state transformation is defined by a system
of partial differential equations (PDEs) which eliminate roll and pitch rate dependence in the trans-
formed image feature kinematics. A method for computing the general solutions of these PDEs is
given, and we show a particular solution reduces to an established virtual camera approach. We treat
point and line cases and introduce image moment features defined in the virtual camera image plane.
Robustness of the control design is improved by accounting for attitude measurement bias, and
uncertainty in thrust gain, mass, and image feature depth. The asymptotic stability of the closed-loop
is proven. The method is based on a simple proportional-integral-derivative (PID) structure which can
be readily implemented on-board. Experimental results show improved performance relative to pre-
vious work.
1. Introduction
Visual servoing involves using computer vision feedback
to help guide a robot’s motion (Chaumette & Hutchinson,
2006). Recently, there has been a particular interest in
applying visual servoing to motion control of unmanned
aerial vehicles (UAVs). Visual servoing can allow a UAV
to explore locations where global positioning system
(GPS) is unavailable (Fraundorfer et al., 2012) or solve
a specific relative positioning task such as automated
landing (Ma, Soatto, Kosecka, & Sastry, 2003). In this
paper, we focus on visual servoing for a quadrotor UAV
with a single onboard downward facing camera, and
the control objective is to regulate the UAV’s relative
displacement and yaw to a visual target defined by point
or line features and located on the ground. Conven-
tionally, a visual seroving law relies on kinematics of
an error signal which depends on an image measure-
ment. The resulting control structure is divided into
two feedback loops (Chaumette & Hutchinson, 2006).
An outer loop uses low-rate video camera feedback to
compute a velocity reference based on the kinematics,
and a high-bandwidth inner loop tracks this reference.
This two-loop structure is natural due to the difference
in sampling rates of the loops. The structure simpli-
fies the control design by assuming perfect inner-loop

tracking. However, as pointed out in Chaumette and

(2014), when high-performance motion control is
required or the system is underactuated, the dynamics of
the robot should be considered. Dynamic visual servoing
directly accounts for the robot’s dynamics and is a chal-
lenging problem due to the nonlinearity of the model.
Visual servoing is normally categorised into two basic
approaches: position-based visual servoing and image-
based visual servoing (IBVS). PBVS methods reconstruct
the vehicle pose from image measurements and control
the motion directly in SE(3), e.g. (Azrad, Kendoul, &
Nonami, 2010; Mejias, Saripalli, Campoy, & Sukhatme,
2006; Shakernia, Ma, Koo, & Sastry, 1999). A benefit of
PBVS is that non-vision based motion controllers can
be used. In this paper, we take an IBVS approach where
the feature error is directly computed from the image
measurements. This avoids three-dimensional (3D)
position reconstruction which requires accurate camera
calibration and a prior 3D model of a target. In general,
IBVS is relatively insensitive to camera calibration error
even though the IBVS law can depend on the camera’s
intrinsic parameters (Espiau, 1994). Another feature of
IBVS is that the target object tends not to leave the field
of view (FoV) since the design minimises image feature
error. However, the image feature kinematics in IBVS

is usually nonlinear and this complicates the control

A number of IBVS approaches have been proposed
for UAVs. The seminal work in Hamel and Mahony
(2002) shows that a perspective projection camera model
destroys the triangular structure of the system dynamics.
Hence, a spherical projection camera model is introduced
to give the image feature kinematics a passivity-like prop-
erty, i.e., the time derivative of the norm of the error func-
tion is independent of angular velocity. By introducing
first-order moment point features, a backstepping con-
troller is proposed. As the features in Hamel and Mahony
(2002) are insensitive to vertical motion due to an ill-
conditioned interaction matrix, work in Bourquardez,
Mahony, Hamel, and Chaumette (2006) modifies them by
rescaling and partitioning. In Bourquardez et al. (2009),
the modified image moment features are applied to visual
servoing of a UAV. A similar approach to Hamel and
Mahony (2002) is in Mahony and Hamel (2005) where
first-order line image moment features are proposed for
applications with linear structure such as following power
lines or pipelines. We remark that in the spherical pro-
jection approach, it is hard to propose a feature to con-
trol yaw. It is well known that a homography matrix
embeds information about the relative pose between
two camera frames (Ma et al., 2003). Hence, in Metni,
Hamel, and Derkx (2005), de Plinval, Morin, Mouyon,
and Hamel (2011), and de Plinval, Morin, Mouyon, and
Hamel (2013) a visual servoing scheme is proposed using
image features extracted from the homography matrix
which maps image points between the desired and cur-
rent image planes. Compared to approaches using spher-
ical moment features, this approach only works for pla-
nar targets. Work in Metni et al. (2005), de Plinval et al.
(2011), and de Plinval et al. (2013) either controls partial
motion of the UAV or is limited to a small range of image
feature error. With the assumption that the image and
target planes remain parallel during motion of the vehi-
cle, point moment features and a virtual spring concept
lead to a design which accounts for the non-passive prop-
erty of the kinematics (Ozawa & Chaumette, 2011, 2013).
The assumption in this approach is potentially restrictive
for more aggressive UAV maneuvers since lateral motion
leads to non-parallel image and target planes. The atti-
tude of a UAV is commonly estimated using an attitude
and heading reference system (AHRS) which is integrated
into most UAV autopilots. Measurement of a vehicle’s atti-
tude makes it possible to define a virtual camera with
zero roll and pitch which can be used for visual servoing,
e.g. Jabbari et al. (2014), Lee, Lim, H., Kim, and Seong
(2012), and Xie, Lynch, and Jagersand (2014). In the vir-
tual camera approach, features used for visual servoing
are computed in the virtual camera frame. The image
kinematics is simpler since it is independent of roll and
pitch rates. In Xie et al. (2014), an adaptive IBVS con-
troller for linear structure is proposed based on a virtual
camera. In Abdessameud and Janabi-Sharifi (2015), a vir-
tual camera with no rotational motion is proposed and an
output feedback controller from Abdessameud and
Tayebi (2010) is modified to achieve visual servoing for a
moving target. Le Bras, Hamel, Mahony, and Treil (2011)
utilise a virtual plane which is parallel to the target plane
and tangent to the unit sphere in the spherical projection.
The area of the convex hull of the image points in the vir-
tual camera and the first-order image moment are used
to form the image feature for visual servoing. An adap-
tive output feedback backstepping controller is developed
which does not require velocity measurement.
In this paper, we adopt a different two-loop struc-
ture for dynamic visual servoing. The outer loop design
is based on the UAV translational dynamics not its
kinematics. The inner loop tracks the reference attitude
signal from the outer loop. The control design is com-
plicated due to the appearance of the derivative of atti-
tude in the outer loop. To eliminate this dependence
in the outer loop, we introduce new state coordinates.
The new state is defined by a system of first-order lin-
ear homogeneous partial differential equations (PDEs)
whose general solution can be obtained. We show the vir-
tual camera approach proposed in Jabbari et al. (2014),
Lee et al. (2012), and Xie et al. (2014) is a particular
solution to the PDEs. Hence, the proposed scheme gen-
eralises the virtual camera approach. A constant rota-
tion is used to generalise the virtual camera in Jabbari
et al. (2014), Lee et al. (2012), and Xie et al. (2014). We
introduce commonly used moment features to simplify
the image feature kinematics so that they have linear
dependence on velocity. The proposed control is robust
to system uncertainty in thrust gain, mass, and image
feature depth. We also account for attitude measurement
bias which leads to non-zero steady-state error as shown
in Fink, Xie, Lynch, and Jagersand (2015). For simplic-
ity of on-board implementation, our approach is based
on a proportional-integral-derivative (PID) structure. We
achieve the same level of robustness as more complex
adaptive methods such as Jabbari et al. (2014), Xie et al.
(2014). Another important contribution of our work
lies in its experimental validation. This should be com-
pared to Jabbari et al. (2014) and Xie et al. (2014) where
only simulation results are provided. To the best of the
authors’ knowledge, this is the first experimental vali-
dation of visual servoing with a virtual camera for line
structures.

3. The state transformation approach
In this section, we justify the inner–outer loop controller
structure used and explain how a state transformation
approach eliminates input derivatives in the outer-loop.
The approach is illustrated on a simple planar vertical
take-off and landing (PVTOL) vehicle. Next, we con-
sider point and line basic measurements, introduce image
moment features in the new states, and provide the result-
ing kinematics.
3.1. General philosophy
As discussed in Section 1, the classical IBVS approach
cannot be applied to an underactuated UAV where the
velocities vc and ωc are not independently controlled. In
this paper, we consider a different two-loop structure as
shown in Figure 2. The inner loop tracks an attitude ref-
erence denoted R∗, and the outer-loop provides this ref-
erence to the inner loop in order to regulate the image
feature error es = s −s∗, where s denotes the image fea-
ture to be chosen below and s∗denotes the desired value
of s. A main reason for adopting this control structure is
that it provides a reliable and simple solution for motion
control. Each loop becomes fully actuated and this sim-
plifies the control design and gain tuning to optimise
performance. A similar two-loop structure for conven-
tional position control is found in many autopilots, e.g.
Godbolt (2013), PX4 (2014). Stability of the closed-loop
system requires the inner loop to have a relatively high
bandwidth. The visual servoing problem naturally fits the
inner–outer loop structure since AHRS measurements
are relatively fast compared to image feedback. Further-
more, the inner loop uses a relatively high-gain control to
ensures closed-loop stability. We discuss the conditions
for stability in Section 4.4.3. One alternative is to use ωc
as the control for the outer loop, e.g. Guenard, Hamel, and
Mahony (2008). However, a benefit of the proposed struc-
ture is its direct control of attitude which ensure attitude
remains in a safe region. It also makes it easier to keep the
target in the camera’s FoV.

in Figure 2 for visual servoing, the outer-loop dynam-

visual servoing of lateral motion. This paper extends the

UAV. From (1a), (2), and (3), the outer loop is

4. Controller design
The objective of IBVS is to minimise an image feature
error es. In this section, we propose a PID controller for
the outer-loop based on the image moment features given
in Section 3. The control design considers system uncer-
tainties and a stability proof is given.
4.1. Uncertainty modelling
During the identification experiments of the UAV’s thrust
model, we observed a slow decrease in thrust gain KT
as the battery voltage dropped during flight. In prac-
tice, the effect of decreased KT is significant as the vehi-
cle looses altitude. To compensate for this variation, we
treat KT as an unknown constant parameter. We remark
that accounting for variation in KT is equivalent to deal-
ing with uncertainty in mass m as the parameters enter
as a ratio in the dynamics (1a). We also observed from
our previous results in Fink et al. (2015) that the image
feature error converges to a non-zero constant. After an
exhaustive search for the source of this error, we con-
cluded the problem is due to Euler angle estimate bias in
the AHRS output. Even small bias (i.e., 1°) leads to signifi-
cant steady-state error. To account for this error, we model
Rθφ as Rθφ = RT

6. Conclusion
We have presented a state transformation-based dynamic
visual servoing scheme for an underactuated quadrotor
UAV. The state transformation is obtained by solving a
system of PDEs. A method for computing the general
solutions is given. A particular solution is given by the
virtual camera approach. A set of image moment fea-
tures in the virtual camera is used to derive a dynamic
visual servoing law. Model uncertainty including thrust
gain and attitude measurement bias is considered in the
visual servoing control design. The proposed PID con-
troller is robust to this uncertainty. In addition, the PID
controller does not require an estimate of depth, which is
typically needed in classical IBVS. An indoor quadrotor
platform is used to validate the proposed control. Exper-
imental results demonstrate improved performance with
lower mean image and position error. An analysis of roll
and pitch bias on the virtual camera transformation error
is provided.
