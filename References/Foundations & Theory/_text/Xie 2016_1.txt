Adaptive Visual Servoing of
UAVs Using a Virtual Camera
HUI XIE
GEOFF FINK, Student Member, IEEE
ALAN F. LYNCH, Member, IEEE
MARTIN JAGERSAND
University of Alberta
Edmonton AB, Canada
We propose an adaptive image-based visual servoing controller
for an unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) that is based on a virtual
camera. The controller regulates relative position and yaw of the
vehicle to a planar target consisting of multiple points. We adopt
image moment features, which simplify the controller’s derivation.
The control is adaptive to various system parameters. We prove
exponential stability of the error dynamics. Experimental results
demonstrate controller performance.

I.
INTRODUCTION
Visual servoing of unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs)
uses computer vision feedback to control vehicle motion.
Visual servoing allows a UAV to ﬂy in GPS-denied
environments [1] or can be applied to a relative
positioning task such as autonomous landing [2]. In this
paper we consider visual servoing of a quadrotor UAV
equipped with a single downward facing camera mounted
on-board. The proposed control enables the vehicle to
regulate its relative displacement and yaw to a static visual
target located on the ground. We assume a planar target
with known orientation. Visual servoing usually is
categorized into two approaches: position-based visual
servoing (PBVS) and image-based visual servoing
(IBVS). A number of researchers have applied PBVS to
UAVs, e.g. [2, 3]. In this paper we focus on IBVS methods
where the error signal es = s – s* to be stabilized is
computed from the difference between a feature vector s,
which directly depends on image plane measurements,
and it’s desired value s*. Since es is computed directly
from image plane measurements, we avoid the need for
3-D position reconstruction used in PBVS. Position
reconstruction requires accurate camera calibration and a
prior 3-D model of the target. In general, IBVS is robust to
camera calibration error [4]. Another feature of IBVS is
the target object tends not to leave the ﬁeld of view (FoV)
since the design minimizes es, which directly depends on
image plane measurements.
A number of researchers have contributed to IBVS
methods for UAVs. One approach chooses image features
which make the system model passive and simplify the
controller design [5–8]. This is because the time derivative
of the norm of the error function depends only on
translational velocity and yaw rate. Popular choices for
point features are image moments computed in a
spherically projected image plane [5]. However, this
feature is insensitive to motion in altitude due to an
ill-conditioned interaction matrix. To overcome this,
modiﬁed spherical image moments were developed [6].
Work similar to [5] for line features is in [7]. Besides
spherical image moments, another feature can be obtained
from the homography matrix between the desired and
current images. This matrix embeds information about the
vehicle’s relative pose [9]. However, these approaches
either control partial motion of the UAV or are limited to a
small range of image feature error. A virtual spring
approach is proposed in [10, 11] to deal with scenarios
where the chosen image feature kinematics do not have
passivity properties. This approach assumes the image and
target planes remain parallel. This assumption is
restrictive since lateral UAV motion leads to a nonparallel
image and target plane. A different approach relies on a
virtual image plane [8, 12–14]. This plane has zero roll
and pitch angle and has the same position and yaw angle
of the real camera frame. In this approach, features in the
real camera image are converted into the virtual camera
image with a geometric transformation which depends on

integrated into most UAV autopilots to stabilize attitude.
The virtual camera transforms the system model such that
its interaction matrix has a decoupled form which
simpliﬁes derivation of the controller. A similar work for

state-transformation approach is proposed which
generalizes the virtual camera method mentioned above.
Due to the simpler model that results, this paper adopts the
virtual camera approach.
The contribution of this paper is to propose an IBVS
law which is adaptive to system uncertainties. We consider
a simple linear input model for quadrotor thrust. We
assume propeller thrust is modelled by the product of a
gain and the square of the physical input: pulse-width
modulation (PWM) voltage to the electronic speed
controller (ESC). We observe from experiments that the
value of the thrust gain gradually reduces as the battery
voltage drops, and the reduction in thrust has a noticeable
effect on UAV motion. Hence, we compensate for
variation in this parameter using adaptive control. From
our ﬂight tests in [15] we observed that image feature
error es converges to a nonzero steady state error due to
constant measurement bias in attitude. Hence, our design
adapts to this measurement error. As in [8] we include
adaptation to uncertainty in mass and desired depth.
Furthermore, we experimentally validate the performance
of the proposed control. This should be compared with
work in [8] where only simulation results are provided. To
the best of our knowledge, this is the ﬁrst time an IBVS
based on a virtual camera approach and adaptive IBVS
have been validated in ﬂight tests.
This paper is organized as follows. Section II describes the
camera model, image features, kinematics of the image
features, and UAV dynamics. Section III describes the
system uncertainty and presents the adaptive IBVS
control. The simulation and experimental results are
shown in Section IV. Finally, concluding remarks are
provided in Section V.
II.
IMAGE-BASED VISUAL SERVOING MODELLING
In this section we ﬁrst introduce reference frames used
in the camera model and the UAV dynamics. Next we
describe the virtual camera approach, select image
moments in the virtual camera image plane as features for
visual servoing, and provide the corresponding image
feature kinematics. Finally, we specify a rigid body UAV
dynamics which is used to derive the IBVS control.

III.
ADAPTIVE CONTROLLER DESIGN
In this section we motivate the use of an inner-outer
loop control structure for visual servoing and describe the
system uncertainty accounted for in the adaptive control.
We present the controller design and prove the asymptotic
stability of the closed loop.
Fig. 3.
Visual servoing with inner-outer control loop structure.
A.
Inner-Outer Loop Control Structure
The control of UAVs is typically divided into an inner
and outer loop structure as shown in Fig. 3. This two-loop
structure is used in this paper and is often used
independent of the sensors employed (e.g. camera or
GPS). The inner loop tracks an orientation reference of the
vehicle, and the outer loop provides a roll, pitch, and yaw
reference to the inner loop in order to achieve control of
translational and yaw motion. The two loop structure
arises in part from a difference in sensor sample rates. It
also provides a reliable solution for motion control found
in many autopilots [20, 21]. In the case of visual servoing,
the decoupled control structure is natural due to the low
frequency at which the camera acquires and processes
images. On the other hand, the relatively fast inertial
measurement unit (IMU) sensors driving the inner attitude
control allow it to run at a higher rate. A theoretical
analysis of the stability of this closed-loop structure is in
[16, 22].
B.

observed that lateral image feature error converged to a
nonzero constant. As conﬁrmed by the work in [16], the
bias errors in Euler angle estimates from the AHRS are the
cause of this image feature error. To account for this error
we model Rθφ as Rθφ = RT

V.
CONCLUSION
We have presented an adaptive IBVS control that uses
image moment features from projected points in a virtual
camera image. The image moment features in the virtual
camera image lead to a simple interaction matrix. The
proof of exponential stability of the error dynamics is
given for the case of target points in a horizontal plane.
The proposed control does not require an estimate of
depth, known mass, and thrust coefﬁcient. The method is
robust to measurement bias in roll and pitch. An indoor
quadrotor platform is used to validate the control. The
results demonstrate improved performance with lower
mean image and position error. We explain how the
proposed control can be extended to the nonhorizontal
target case for motion control in the translational degrees
of freedom. Future work involves developing an output
feedback controller to remove the requirement of velocity
measurement. Also, achieving regulation of yaw for the
nonhorizontal target plane case is planned.
