Autonomous takeoff and landing are crucial for unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) to perform various 
missions automatically. However, navigation sensors with low accuracy such as global positioning system 
(GPS) are limited in autonomous takeoff and landing for marine applications when ships are moving 
fast and oscillating by sea waves. In this study, an image-based visual servoing (IBVS) technique, which 
was originally used to land a UAV on a static target, is extended for a moving target. In particular, the 
ship velocity is estimated and used as a feed-forward term in the IBVS controller. To accurately estimate 
the velocity of the ship, velocity data from the GPS on the ship, image information obtained from the 
camera in the UAV, and dynamic model of the ship are combined using the Kalman ﬁlter framework. 
Besides, considering the under-actuated nature of the UAV and oscillation of the ship, a virtual plane 
concept, adaptive IBVS gain and feature shape compensation are introduced. Lastly, to apply the IBVS 
controller to the moving ship deck landing, a robust and safe autonomous landing procedure, starting 
from the approach to the touchdown phase, is also developed. The proposed autonomous landing system 
is validated via simulations and various real-world ﬂight experiments simulating situations in which the 
ship is moving fast and oscillated by sea waves. In the ﬂight experiments, the UAV lands successfully on 
the landing pad with the average touchdown error of 0.2 m while the ship is oscillating at the Sea State
4 and is moving faster than 5 m/s.

1. Introduction
Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) have been widely used for 
surveillance, reconnaissance missions, search and rescue opera-
tions, and wind turbine and bridge inspections in both military 
and industrial ﬁelds [1–5]. For the aforementioned missions, the 
takeoff and landing of the UAV are better to be performed au-
tonomously before and after missions. In particular, if the UAV 
is operated at marine environments far from the land, the UAV 
should be able to land on possibly small and narrow areas of a 
moving ship oscillated by sea waves [6]. However, owing to sig-
niﬁcant position errors, it is insuﬃcient to perform autonomous 
landing in this condition using conventional sensing systems such 
as global positioning system (GPS). Although real time kinematic 
GPS (RTK-GPS) can be employed for accurate position estimations 
[7], installing RTK-GPS on a moving ship is expensive and challeng-
ing. To address the inaccuracy of the GPS, vision sensors can be

There are many researches about autonomous landing of a UAV

to improve the landing performance, reliable velocity estimations

is made fully autonomous.

2. Related work
Visual servoing is a control method for guiding unmanned 
vehicles or robots (especially robot manipulators [10–12]) to a 
target position using a vision sensor. It can be categorized into 
position-based visual servoing (PBVS) and image-based visual ser-
voing (IBVS) [13]. In PBVS, states are deﬁned as the pose of the 
target, and the target pose is estimated with respect to the camera 
frame. The error is expressed as the relative pose between the tar-
get and camera, and the PBVS outputs the command to reduce the 
error. PBVS enables the camera to move to the target in an opti-
mal trajectory. However, poor state estimation could destabilize the 
pose of the camera, which triggers issues such as perturbations in 
the trajectory and inaccuracies after convergence [14].
Several studies have been conducted on the autonomous land-
ing of UAVs using PBVS. Jung et al. estimated the horizontal 
distance error between the landing target and the UAV using 
the center of the measured feature position and marker length 
information [15]. For the autonomous landing on the target, 
a proportional–integral–derivative controller was employed. To 
handle the limited ﬁeld of view (FOV) of the camera, Chen et al. 
used a pan-tilt camera, estimated the landing target, and then con-
ducted autonomous landing [16]. Yang et al. exploited PBVS to 
achieve the takeoff and landing of a UAV with a square-root un-
scented Kalman ﬁlter to estimate the pose of the UAV [17]. Zhao 
et al. proposed the PBVS controller that is robust to the time delays 
in the translational and rotational dynamics [18]. Acevedo et al. 
conducted a study to achieve landing on a moving platform us-
ing PBVS [19] where the platform moved with a maximum speed 
of 10 km/h. Falanga et al. developed a fully autonomous quadrotor 
system that can land on a moving target using PBVS [20]. In the 
study conducted by Santana et al., autonomous landing using PBVS 
on a platform oscillating with the heave motion was presented 
[21], and simulations were carried out with the virtual robot ex-
perimentation platform (V-REP) [22] to verify the algorithm.
In contrast, in IBVS, states are deﬁned as the position of the 
features in the image plane, and the error is deﬁned as the pixel 
position error between the desired and current feature positions. 
From the pixel error in the image plane, the desired velocity com-
mand to move the camera to a target pose is calculated with image 
Jacobian, which explains the relationship between the velocity of 
the camera in 3-D space and feature velocity in 2-D image plane. 
IBVS is known to be more robust against pixel measurement errors 
than PBVS [14].
Several studies have also been actively conducted on au-
tonomous landing using IBVS. Tang et al. applied spherical image 
centroids-based IBVS for going through a window and landing on 
a target for a UAV [23]. Hamel et al. applied IBVS to an under-
actuated system for the ﬁrst time using a robust backstepping 
technique [24]. They considered the full dynamics of the camera 
motion ﬁxed to the rigid body. As an extension of this work, Gue-
nard et al. carried out the hovering experiment of a quadrotor UAV 
[25]. Lee et al. applied a virtual image plane to IBVS to compensate 
the effect of the attitude of the UAV. Furthermore, they designed 
an adaptive sliding mode controller [26,27] in order to keep the 
image within the FOV of the camera. Especially, in [27], Patrol 
mode which is to ﬁnd out the target is added. Serra et al. proposed 
a control law for landing on a platform with the heave motion, and 
then conducted simulations and indoor experiments [28]. In the 
research conducted by Truong et al. [29], a controller was intro-
duced for the ship landing of an helicopter, using a combination of 
IBVS and translational rate command, and then simulations were 
carried out. In the study by Rakotomamonjy et al. [30], to land on 
a moving ship deck, the velocity of the ship was estimated using 
the response amplitude operator and the autoregressive moving 
average model. The motion of the ship was compensated in the 
IBVS controller and its performance was veriﬁed via simulations. 
Borshchova et al. conducted autonomous landing simulations and 
experiments on a ship deck [31,32]. They adopted the color detec-
tion method as features for IBVS to reduce the computational load. 
Simulations were also conducted for a moving target with V-REP 
simulations. Wynn et al. proposed feed-forward IBVS (FF-IBVS) to 
compensate the velocity of the moving ship [8]. The velocity of 
the ship was estimated using an extended Kalman ﬁlter (EKF), 
which fuses visual and GPS measurements, while the estimated 
velocity was used as a feed-forward term combined with the IBVS 
controller. They also proposed the entire process for autonomous 
landing on a moving target starting from the approach phase. In 
the experiment, the velocity of the target was set at approximately 
1 m/s, with the heave motion; in addition, precision landing per-
formance was veriﬁed in ﬂight experiments.
As mentioned in the above paragraphs, there are existing stud-
ies to land the UAV on a moving ship. However, there are few 
studies dealing with the harsh outdoor environment where the 
landing pad is oscillated by sea waves as well as going forward at 
a high speed. For the autonomous landing system, the entire land-
ing procedure starting from the approach phase to touchdown is 
also not studied suﬃciently. Therefore, in this paper, ﬁrst, to land 
the UAV on the ship moving fast and oscillating, several techniques 
such as adaptive-IBVS gain, feature shape compensation and sen-
sor fusion are exploited. Second, for robust and safe autonomous 
landing, the entire landing procedure based on the state machine 
is designed. Lastly, to verify the performance of the autonomous

4. Feed-forward IBVS
The conventional IBVS assumes that the target is stationary 
[13,14]. To apply the IBVS to a moving target, the target velocity 
should be explicitly considered. In this study, the horizontal linear 
velocity of the moving ship is used. The desired velocity command 
from IBVS combined with the target velocity compensation as a 
feed-forward term makes a new command for feed-forward IBVS 
(FF-IBVS) as:
vd, f f = vd,4D O F + ˆvtarget
(10)
where ˆvtarget = [ˆvtarget,x ˆvtarget,y 0 0]⊤is the estimated horizontal 
velocity of the ship.
We employ the information from the GPS and camera for the 
target velocity estimation. The GPS is attached to the ship deck and 
measures the position and velocity of the ship with the respect to 
the world frame. Then, these measurements are transformed into 
the relative values with respect to the UAV using the information 
from the GPS onboard the UAV. Meanwhile, the camera calculates 
the relative pose of the landing pad on the ship relative to the 
UAV using the features captured in the image plane. From these 
relative sensing data, the state of the landing pad relative to the 
UAV is estimated by the Kalman ﬁlter (KF). Besides, to facilitate the 
information from the two different sensors, we apply the track-to-
track fusion algorithm [33] as presented in Fig. 5. The algorithm 
comprises two distinct KFs updating the ship state via the GPS 
and camera, and they are fused accordingly. Note that the posi-
tion measurements from the camera are relatively accurate and its 
update rate is faster compared with GPS. However, camera mea-
surements may not be available when the features are out of the 
camera ﬁeld-of-view (FOV). Thus, by fusing these two sensors with 
Kalman ﬁlters, we can make the estimation more robust against 
intermittent measurement loss of camera or GPS as well as im-
proving the estimation accuracy.

5. Autonomous ship deck landing system
This section describes the autonomous landing system that ex-
ploits the feed-forward IBVS algorithm. The entire autonomous 
ship deck landing system is illustrated in Fig. 7. The ship is as-
sumed to oscillate with six-DOF motions (roll, pitch, yaw, surge, 
sway, and heave) generated by sea waves and also move forward. 
The landing pad is positioned at the stern of the ship, and the GPS 
is installed on the landing pad. The multiple markers are placed 
on the landing pad and are used as the features for IBVS. The mis-
sion of the UAV is to land on the ship deck using the GPS and the 
on-board camera of the UAV.
The markers used in this research are illustrated in Fig. 8. We 
designed three IBVS levels to enable the autonomous visual land-
ing from the high altitude where different marker sizes were used 
for each IBVS level. At a high altitude, small markers cannot be 
detected owing to the limited camera resolution, and at a low al-
titude, large markers can be out of FOV. For the markers, the AR 
tags, especially the ArUco markers, are exploited, which can be de-
tected from a distant camera in a highly reliable manner [38]. The 
center points of AR tags at IBVS levels 1 and 2 form a square with 
lengths of 2.2 m and 0.68 m respectively. The marker for IBVS 
level 3 is located at the center of the landing pad. In a 3-D space, 
more than three feature points are required for the IBVS and we 
use four feature points. After the detection of AR tags, the cen-
ter points of four AR tags for IBVS levels 1 and 2 or every corner 
points of the single AR tag for IBVS level 3 are used as feature 
points for IBVS.
Fig. 9 describes the entire landing procedure where the decision 
making for autonomous landing is carried out by the state machine 
structure. There are seven states: approach, three IBVS states (lev-
els 1, 2 and 3), hold, rising and landing. At the approach state, the 
UAV is guided to an approach point of 12 m above the center of 
the landing pad using the GPS information. Once the UAV reaches 
the approach point and AR tags for IBVS level 1 are detected, the 
approach ﬂag becomes true, and FF-IBVS is started. There are three 
IBVS states which include IBVS levels 1, 2 and 3. For each IBVS

continuously for more than 5 s, all parameters are initialized and 
the state is changed back to the approach state.

6.1. Adaptive-gain IBVS
To verify the effect of the adaptive IBVS gain, two simulation 
results, which are with and without the adaptive gain, are com-
pared. Fig. 12 describes the distance between the center of the 
features and the center of the image plane (i.e., the value c in Eq. 
(9)). Whenever features are detected, the red circle is marked on 
the graph. If the adaptive IBVS gain is not applied, the IBVS con-
trol command makes the UAV descend its altitude regardless of the 
horizontal error, resulting in that the features get out of the image 
plane from 15 to 20 s and from 25 to 32 s. On the other hand, with 
the adaptive IBVS gain, when the features are far from the center 
of the image plane, a small control input is applied to the altitude 
direction (i.e., small descending rate); this allows a suﬃcient time 
for the features to get closer to the center of the image plane. The 
simulation results demonstrate that less features are missed with 
the use of the adaptive IBVS gain during the landing process.
6.2. Square compensation for landing pad oscillation
In this subsection, the effect of the square compensation of 
features (illustrated in Fig. 4) is veriﬁed. The simulation environ-
ment is set to the situation where the landing pad performs the 
rotational motion (i.e., rolling, pitching, and yawing) with the am-
plitude and period described in the Table 1. The position errors 
with and without square compensation were compared during the 
simulation of IBVS level 3 for 30 seconds. As shown in Fig. 13, only 
a slight position error exists when square compensation is used, 
whereas the UAV oscillates according to the motion of the landing 
pad when the compensation is not conducted. The mean values of 
the horizontal position error with and without compensation are 
0.0694 m and 0.2237 m, respectively.

8. Conclusions and future work
This study proposed a vision-based autonomous ship deck land-
ing strategy using feed-forward image-based visual servoing (FF-
IBVS). Conventional IBVS schemes cannot guarantee the conver-
gence of the position error to zero if the target is not stationary. 
To resolve this issue, the velocity of the ship was added as a feed-
forward term in IBVS. The motion of the ship was measured by 
the GPS on the ship deck and the camera attached to the UAV, 
and then estimated via Kalman ﬁltering. In addition, the adaptive 
IBVS gain was used, such that the features remained in the FOV, 
and the features were compensated to ﬁt a square to avoid the tilt 
effects of the ship. To accomplish the entire landing procedure au-
tonomously, a landing scheme in the form of the state machine 
structure, including the approach, three IBVS levels according to 
the relative altitude between the ship and the UAV, hold, rising, 
and landing states, was designed. The proposed autonomous land-
ing algorithm was veriﬁed via various simulations and real ﬂight 
experiments.
The method proposed in this paper, the use of additional sen-
sors (i.e., GPS on the ship deck) was necessary to estimate the 
velocity of the ship accurately. However, the sensor attached to the 
ship could complicate the system, and if issues such as communi-
cation failure occurs, autonomous landing might not be possible. 
Therefore, as a future work, a method for estimating the velocity 
of a ship without the aid of GPS will be investigated. In addition 
to that, to cope with the strong wind and gusts in harsh marine 
environments, robust control techniques (e.g., sliding mode con-
trol and/or disturbance observer) will be considered as the future 
work.
